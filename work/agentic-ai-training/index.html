Teaching teams to build their own AI teacher

Internal training that turned agentic workflows and prompt engineering into practical tools for non-technical departments, centred on a Socratic teacher with a syllabus and a grading rubric.

TrainingAgentic workflowsPrompt engineeringSocratic method

Problem

Agentic workflows and prompt engineering are unfamiliar outside engineering. Explaining them in engineers' terms doesn't help a finance or HR team decide what to try on Monday.

Process

I designed and delivered the training myself, translating the concepts into practical, department-level applications for non-technical teams across the company. Each session started from the team's own work rather than mine.

The Socratic teacher

The centrepiece was a Socratic teacher: an AI teacher that people from other departments learned to build for any subject they wanted to master.

Each one has three parts:

  • A syllabus: what is to be learned, and in what order.
  • A rubric: what a good answer looks like.
  • The Socratic method: instead of lecturing, it teaches by asking questions, and it grades your answers against the rubric.

The learner works through the syllabus at their own pace and gets honest feedback along the way, so they learn faster and in the order that suits them.

Why it worked for non-technical teams

Nobody in the room needed to code. They already knew their own subject, so the skill they were really learning was how to write down a syllabus and a rubric clearly enough for an AI to teach and grade from. That is prompt engineering in practice, applied to something they cared about.

Result

Teams outside engineering left with a working picture of what agentic tooling can and cannot do, and with a teacher they built themselves. It is the same instinct as the agent system, applied to people: make the boundaries clear, then make the tool usable.

Employer systems, repositories, work items and client names are deliberately left out. The mechanism is the point.
